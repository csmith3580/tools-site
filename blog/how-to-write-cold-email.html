How to Write a Cold Email That Gets Replies (With Examples)

By the Ignitool team July 14, 2026 8 min read

The average cold email gets a reply rate of around 1–3%. The best ones — written by people who understand what actually works — get 10–20% or more. The gap between those numbers isn't talent. It's knowing which rules to follow and which conventional wisdom to ignore.

Why most cold emails fail

Most cold emails fail for one of three reasons:

  1. They're about the sender, not the recipient. "I'm John from Acme, and we help companies like yours..." The reader stopped caring after "I'm."
  2. They're too long. A cold email is not a sales brochure. If it takes more than 30 seconds to read, most people won't.
  3. The ask is too big. Asking a stranger for a 45-minute call on the first email is like asking someone to marry you on a first date.

The emails that work flip all three of these on their head. They lead with the recipient, they're short, and they ask for something small.

The anatomy of a cold email that converts

Every high-performing cold email has the same basic structure. Five elements, in this order:

1. A subject line that earns the open

Your subject line has one job: get the email opened. Not sell the product, not explain the offer — just get opened. The best cold email subject lines are short (under 7 words), specific, and feel personal rather than broadcast.

❌ Generic — low open rate

"Quick question about your marketing strategy"
"Following up on my previous email"
"Exciting opportunity for [Company Name]"

✅ Specific — higher open rate

"Your LinkedIn post on remote hiring"
"Question about Acme's Q3 expansion"
"Saw you're hiring 3 SDRs"

2. A personalized opening line

The first line of your email must be about them — something specific you noticed about their company, their work, or their recent activity. This is the line that separates a cold email from spam.

It doesn't need to be elaborate. One sentence that demonstrates you actually looked at their LinkedIn or company website is enough to earn the next few seconds of attention.

3. A one-sentence value proposition

Tell them what you do in one sentence, framed around the benefit to them — not the features of your product. "We help B2B SaaS companies reduce churn by identifying at-risk customers before they cancel" beats "We're a customer success platform with AI-powered analytics" every time.

4. Social proof or a specific result

One specific, credible claim is worth more than a paragraph of general statements. A number, a company name, or a concrete outcome gives the reader a reason to believe you can deliver what you're promising.

5. A low-friction ask

Don't ask for a 30-minute call. Ask a yes-or-no question, or ask if they're the right person to talk to. Small asks get answered. Big asks get deleted.

A cold email that works — full example

Subject: Your recent LinkedIn post on onboarding

Hi Sarah,

I saw your post about how new hires at Acme take 90 days to ramp — that's a real challenge in a remote-first environment.

We help SaaS companies cut that ramp time in half by automating the first 30 days of onboarding. Lattice used us to go from 90 to 44 days last quarter.

Worth a quick conversation? Even a yes/no on whether this is a priority right now would help.

— Mark

Count the words: 79. Count the sentences: 5. The personalized opener, the value prop, the social proof, and the low-friction ask are all there. Nothing else.

The follow-up sequence

Most replies don't come from the first email. They come from follow-ups — because people are busy, not because they're not interested. A three-email sequence is standard:

  • Email 1: The full cold email above
  • Email 2 (3 days later): A one-line bump — "Wanted to make sure this didn't get buried. Happy to send more details if helpful."
  • Email 3 (7 days later): A breakup email — "I'll stop reaching out after this one. If the timing's ever right, my contact info is below."

The breakup email often gets the highest reply rate of the three — people respond to closure.

What not to do

  • Don't open with "I hope this finds you well." It's the single most overused opening in cold email. It signals a template, not a human.
  • Don't use "just" or "quickly." "I just wanted to quickly reach out..." communicates low confidence. Own the outreach.
  • Don't attach anything to a cold email. Attachments in cold emails go to spam — and look suspicious to recipients who don't know you.
  • Don't ask multiple questions. One ask, one answer. Multiple questions create decision paralysis and get no reply.
  • Don't send from a brand-new domain. Cold emails from new domains have poor deliverability. Warm up your domain before sending at volume.
